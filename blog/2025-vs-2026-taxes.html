What actually changed between your 2025 and 2026 taxes.

Skip the headline recaps. These are the seven changes that move real paychecks, with the exact figures and exactly who each one touches. Every number is sourced from IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 and its 2025 counterpart — the same dataset that powers our tax calculator, which now computes both years side by side.

1. The standard deduction climbs — again

Single filers: $15,750 → $16,100. Married filing jointly: $31,500 → $32,200. That's roughly $350–700 more income taxed at 0% before you've done anything clever. Who it touches: the ~90% of filers who don't itemize.

2. Every bracket edge moves up ~2.3%

The 22% bracket now starts at $50,400 (single) instead of $48,475; the 24% at $105,700 instead of $103,350, and so on. If your raise was under ~2.3%, you may pay a lower effective rate in 2026 than 2025 on essentially the same real income. Who it touches: everyone with wage growth near or below inflation.

3. The Social Security wage base jumps $8,400

$176,100 → $184,500. Earn above the old cap and you'll pay up to $521 more in Social Security tax (6.2% × $8,400) — twice that if self-employed. This is the quiet tax increase nobody mentions in the bracket headlines. Who it touches: incomes above ~$176k.

4. Charitable giving without itemizing — new and permanent

From 2026, cash gifts are deductible up to $1,000 single / $2,000 joint even on the standard deduction. In 2025, non-itemizers got nothing. Who it touches: anyone who gives and doesn't itemize — which was previously a "no tax benefit" combination.

5. …but itemized charity grows a floor

Also new in 2026: itemizers can only deduct giving above 0.5% of AGI. Give $3,000 on a $200,000 income and the first $1,000 no longer counts. Who it touches: itemizers with modest giving; large donors barely notice.

6. The SALT cap inches up, and the phase-down moves

$40,000 → $40,400, with the income phase-down starting at $505,000 (was $500,000), shrinking back toward the old $10,000 floor at 30¢ per dollar above it. Who it touches: itemizers in high-tax states; painfully, those with incomes just past half a million.

7. The temporary deductions keep ticking

Tips (to $25,000), overtime premium (to $12,500/$25,000), car-loan interest (to $10,000) and the $6,000 senior bonus all apply in both 2025 and 2026 — but all four expire after 2028. Two of their four years are now spent. Who it touches: service workers, hourly workers with overtime, new-car buyers, and 65+ filers — who should plan as if these are temporary, because they are.

See your own delta, not the averages

Averages hide everything interesting: the wage-base jump can swamp the bracket relief for one household while the charity change flips the answer for another. The take-home calculator has a 2025/2026 toggle and always shows your gap between the two years' rules — one number, your situation, no forms.

Figures: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 and Rev. Proc. 2024-40 (as amended by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act). Planning estimates, not tax advice.
