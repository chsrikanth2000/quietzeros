The same $50,000, three different mortgages.

A windfall lands — inheritance, bonus, sold the boat. You point it at the mortgage. What happens next depends entirely on which lever you pull, and almost nobody explains the difference.

Take a concrete loan: $400,000 at 6.5% for 30 years — a $2,528.27 payment, and if you just make the payments, $510,178 of lifetime interest. Now drop $50,000 on it two years in, three different ways. Every number below comes from the Mortgage Lab; you can rebuild each scenario yourself in about a minute.

Lever 1 — extra principal payment: buys time

You send the $50,000 as an ordinary extra payment. Your monthly bill doesn't change. What changes is the clock: the loan now dies in month 267 instead of 360 — 7¾ years early — and total interest falls to $323,570. The lump sum saved $186,608.

Read that against the check you wrote: every dollar of the lump sum eliminated $3.73 of interest. No investment product legally sold to the public guarantees that.

Lever 2 — recast: buys breathing room

Same $50,000, but you ask the lender to recast: they take the lump sum, then re-amortize what's left over the loan's original schedule. Your payment drops from $2,528 to about $2,205 — $324/month of permanent relief — but the payoff date doesn't move, and the interest saved is $58,699. Less than a third of lever 1.

That's not a mistake — it's a different product. Recast when cash flow is the problem (new baby, one income for a while). Prepay when total cost is the problem. Most lenders charge $150–500 for a recast and want at least $5,000–10,000; almost nobody advertises it, because it's the cheapest thing they offer.

Lever 3 — refinance: changes the rate itself

The third lever doesn't need a windfall at all — it needs rates to have dropped. Refinancing rewrites the price of every remaining dollar, but resets the clock and charges closing costs, which is why a lower payment can still cost more over a lifetime. The honest arithmetic — break-even month, term-reset penalty, and the keep-your-old-payment escape — is its own topic: the refinance calculator does it in full.

The part that generalizes

Timing dominates. The same $50,000 that saves $186,608 in year two saves only $37,211 in year twenty — the identical action, one-fifth the effect, because early principal stops compounding against you for decades and late principal doesn't. If a windfall is coming and the mortgage is the plan, sooner isn't slightly better. It's five times better.
